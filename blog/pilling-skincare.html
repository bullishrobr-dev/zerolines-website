Pilling: Why Your Skincare Rolls Off in Little Grey Balls

Last updated: 23 November 2026 7 min read Free skin analysis · 4 min

You applied everything in the right order. Cleanser, serum, moisturiser, sunscreen. Then you smoothed on the last layer and your face began shedding small grey worms of product that gather along the jaw and at the hairline. It reads like a verdict, as though your skin had assessed the whole routine and handed it back. It has not. Pilling is a mechanical problem rather than a biological one, and mechanical problems have unglamorous, reliable fixes.

In short

  • The little grey balls are not dead skin. Roll one between your fingers and it smears — it is product, mostly a film-former that had not finished setting or had nothing to grip underneath.
  • Two causes account for almost all of it: too much product, and not enough time. A film has a thickness it can usefully hold, and its setting depends on evaporation, which depends on the room you are standing in.
  • The fixes in order: use half as much, wait sixty to ninety seconds and press rather than sweep, leave the steamy bathroom, and change the order before you change any product.
  • Sunscreen is where it shows worst, because it is the most film-dependent thing most people own and it goes on last. This is the one place not to use less — thin what is underneath it instead.

What the little grey balls actually are

They are not, for the most part, your skin. That is worth clearing up first, because the folk explanation — that pilling is dead skin being lifted away, proof the product is doing something — is one of skincare's more durable myths. Roll one of the balls between your fingers. It smears. It is product.

The grey comes from a mixture of things: the way a thin film scatters light once it has been rolled into a lump, a small amount of surface debris it has gathered on the way, sometimes a fibre from a towel or a jumper. But the bulk of what has come off your face is what you put on it, perhaps ninety seconds ago.

Most modern cosmetics contain at least one ingredient whose entire purpose is to form a continuous film. Silicones such as dimethicone. Film-forming polymers, usually acrylates of some kind. Thickeners and gums — xanthan gum, carbomer, cellulose derivatives. These sit dispersed in water or a volatile solvent while the product is in the bottle, and as that liquid evaporates on your skin they knit together into a very thin, continuous, flexible layer. That layer is why a serum feels silky rather than sticky and why a sunscreen spreads evenly.

A film that has not finished setting is soft. A film that has set but has nothing much to grip underneath is loose. In either state, friction gathers it up on itself, the way dried glue rolls off a palm or an eraser crumbles into worms. The pill is the film, rolled.

Too much product, not enough time

Two causes account for the overwhelming majority of cases, and neither of them is the product being wrong.

The first is quantity. A film has a thickness it can usefully hold, and beyond that the excess simply sits on top, unbonded, waiting to be disturbed. Most people apply considerably more than they need — three or four pumps of a serum where a few drops would cover the face, a fingertip's worth of cream where half of that would do. The extra is not absorbed. It waits.

The second is time. The setting of a film depends on evaporation, and evaporation depends on the room. In a bathroom still warm and wet from a shower, with the mirror fogged, almost nothing evaporates. Sixty seconds in that room is not sixty seconds in a dry bedroom. This is why people report that a routine pills in winter with the heating on full, or in August in Marbella, and behaves impeccably the rest of the year. The formula did not change. The air did.

The fixes, in the order that usually works

Work through these in sequence. The cheap fixes are also the ones that work most often, which is a pleasant coincidence and not a common one.

Use less. Halve whatever you are currently applying and see what happens. If you want to be rigorous about it, do one side of your face at the reduced amount and the other as usual, then look in the mirror ten minutes later. It is an unglamorous experiment and it settles the question quickly.

Wait longer, and stop rubbing. Give each layer sixty to ninety seconds in a room with dry air, and apply by pressing and patting rather than sweeping the fingers back and forth. Sideways friction is the actual mechanism of pilling; pressure alone rarely causes it. Leaving the bathroom after cleansing is, genuinely, one of the more effective things you can do.

Change the order before you change the products. The classic failure is water-heavy over silicone-heavy — a hydrating essence or gel applied on top of something that has already formed a silicone film will bead, sit on the surface and roll at the first touch. The general order in which you apply things does more work here than people expect: thinner and water-based first, richer and oil- or silicone-based after, sealing layers last.

Only then consider changing a product, and change one. If you want to make an informed guess about which one, it helps to be able to read an ingredient list properly — dimethicone and its relatives, anything ending in acrylates crosspolymer, carbomer, and the cellulose gums are the usual participants. A product with several of them near the top of the list is a likelier culprit than one with none.

Why it shows up worst under sunscreen and make-up

Sunscreen is where pilling is most visible and most annoying, and there is a structural reason for it. Sunscreen is the most film-dependent product most people own. It needs film-formers to spread evenly and stay put, it needs to be applied generously to work as intended, and it goes on last, over everything else you have already laid down. Thick layer, applied over other layers, at the end. The arithmetic is against it.

This is the one place where the advice to use less does not apply. Do not thin your sunscreen to stop it pilling — thin what is underneath it instead, or wait longer before it, or accept a lighter moisturiser. An even sunscreen film matters, and a face that has had half its sunscreen wiped off along the jaw is not wearing what it thinks it is wearing. That is the one reason to care about pilling beyond how it looks.

Make-up is the other flashpoint, for the same reason plus one more: a brush or a sponge drags. Foundation is pigment suspended in a film system, applied with an implement designed to move product sideways across the face. It finds every loose layer beneath it. Note also that this is almost exclusively a morning problem — a morning routine has to carry sunscreen and often make-up on top of everything else, where an evening one ends at the last cream and is then left alone.

Why the same two products pill on you and not on your friend

This is the question that arrives most often, usually with some frustration attached: the same two products, the same brands, and someone else has no trouble at all.

Part of it is the surface. Film adheres to what is beneath it, and what is beneath it varies. Skin that is flaking gives the film loose surface cells to bond to, and those come away with it. Skin carrying a lot of oil can prevent a film from setting properly at all. Fine facial hair, which everyone has, gives a partly-set film something to gather around.

Part of it is the air. A humectant-heavy serum stays tacky for longer in humid conditions because it has ambient water to hold, and tacky layers grab whatever goes on next. The same serum in a dry room behaves quite differently — humectants behave differently when the air is dry.

Part of it is simply technique. Pressure, direction, speed, how many passes. Two people applying an identical pair of products will not apply them identically, and the difference between pressing and sweeping is the difference between a film that stays and a film that rolls.

And part of it is the age of what is in the bottle. An emulsion that has begun to separate, or a gel that has thickened over a couple of years on a shelf, will not lay down the film it did when new. If a combination that was fine for a year has recently started misbehaving, it is worth considering whether the product has been open rather a long time.

What pilling does not mean

Pilling is not an allergy. It is not your skin rejecting a formula, or purging, or telling you anything about whether an ingredient suits you. It is not evidence that a product is cheap or fake or badly made. It is, mostly, evidence that something in the routine contains a competent film-former — the same property that makes a sunscreen stay on in the sea and a serum feel like silk rather than water.

Silicones in particular attract a suspicion they have not earned. They are inert, they sit on the surface, and they are used because they do a specific job well. Disliking how they feel is a legitimate preference. Believing they are the reason a routine pills is often correct. Believing that makes them harmful is not.

Here is the honest limit of all this. There is no test that will tell you in advance which two products will pill on your face. A formulator can predict some incompatibilities on paper and still be surprised by what happens on a real person, in a real bathroom, with real hands and a particular humidity. The only reliable method is to change one variable at a time, starting with the free ones.

What we can and cannot say about our own textures

A formulation can be designed to make pilling less likely — fewer competing film systems, textures intended to sit together rather than fight, a sensible progression of weights across a routine. That is a design goal, not a promise: anything containing a film-former can be made to pill with enough product and enough rubbing, and any brand telling you otherwise is describing an ideal rather than a face.

Zero Lines has nothing on sale yet, so we have nothing to tell you about how our own textures will behave on your skin. The great majority of pilling is fixed without buying anything — less product, more patience, a different order. That is an awkward thing for a skincare house to write down. It is also true, and we would rather be the people who told you.
