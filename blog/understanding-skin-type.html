Getting your skin type right matters because it determines everything else: which products you use, the order you layer them in, and how often. Get it wrong, and even the best products can underperform or cause problems.

Why Is Skin Type So Hard to Self-Diagnose?

Skin type isn't a fixed characteristic like blood type. It changes with:

  • Age: Most people produce less oil as they age
  • Season: Winter dryness can make oily skin appear normal, and normal skin appear dry
  • Hormones: Menstrual cycles, pregnancy, and menopause all shift oil production
  • Medications: Retinoids, hormonal contraceptives, and some antidepressants alter skin behaviour
  • Current products: Over-exfoliation can make any skin type appear sensitive; heavy moisturisers can make normal skin seem oily

All this movement means an assessment based on how your skin feels right now — or how it felt five years ago — is often wrong.

What Are the Four (Actually Five) Skin Types?

Dermatologists classify skin into four primary types, with a fifth condition that can overlap with any of them:

Normal

Balanced oil production, minimal sensitivity, small pores, even tone. This is the baseline that most skincare is benchmarked against. In reality, "normal" skin is increasingly rare in adults over 25.

Dry

Low sebum production, tight feeling after cleansing, visible flaking, fine lines that appear deeper than they are. True dry skin is genetic — it's not caused by dehydration (though dehydration makes it worse).

Oily

Excess sebum production, shiny appearance by midday, enlarged pores, tendency toward congestion and breakouts. Oily skin is also largely genetic, though it can be exacerbated by hormones and certain products.

Combination

Oily in the T-zone (forehead, nose, chin) and normal or dry on the cheeks. This is the most common skin type in adults — and the most confusing to treat, since different areas need different care.

Sensitive (A Condition, Not a Type)

Reactive skin that stings, flushes, or develops rashes in response to products, temperature changes, or stress. Sensitivity can occur with any of the four primary types. It's often caused or worsened by barrier damage rather than being purely genetic.

What Makes the Analyser Different from a Skin Quiz?

Most skin type quizzes ask subjective questions: "How does your skin feel at 3 PM?" "Do you experience tightness after cleansing?" These questions are vulnerable to bias, recall error, and the influence of current products.

The Zero Lines Analyser combines computer vision with structured data. It doesn't ask how your skin feels. It looks at how your skin actually appears. The questionnaire adds context; the photo supplies the objectivity.

Over 2,000 people have used the analyser to date. The most common revelation? "I thought I had oily skin, but I'm actually dehydrated." Once that distinction is made, the entire approach to skincare changes.

How Often Should You Reassess Your Skin Type?

We recommend re-analysing your skin every 3–6 months, or whenever you notice a real shift. Skin type isn't fixed. It moves with age, environment, hormones and lifestyle, so a protocol that worked perfectly in summer might need adjusting for winter, and a routine that suited you at 35 may need updating at 45.

The analyser tracks changes over time, showing you how your skin responds to the protocol and when adjustments are needed.

Why Does Skin Type Change?

Your skin type at 20 is not your skin type at 40. Hormonal shifts (puberty, pregnancy, menopause) alter sebum production. Ageing reduces barrier function, making previously resilient skin more sensitive. A change of climate, new medication, and stress can all shift skin behaviour. Even your products play a part: aggressive cleansers can turn oily skin dehydrated, and heavy moisturisers can leave normal skin sluggish.

This is why a skin type assigned once and never revisited eventually stops being true. Skin is dynamic. Skincare has to keep up.

Are There More Than Four Skin Types?

The traditional four-type system (oily, dry, combination, normal) is a useful starting point but dramatically oversimplified. Modern dermatology recognises additional dimensions: sensitivity (reactive vs. resilient), pigmentation (prone to dark spots vs. not), and wrinkling (tendency to form lines vs. not). These dimensions interact — someone can be oily and sensitive, or dry and pigmentation-prone.

The Zero Lines AI Skin Analyser evaluates several dimensions at once: oil production, hydration, sensitivity, pigmentation, wrinkle depth, pore size, and texture. The result is a detailed map of your skin's current state and trajectory, rather than a single label.
