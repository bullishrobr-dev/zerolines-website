Hands and Neck: The Two Places That Show Age First

Last updated: 21 December 2026 8 min read Free skin analysis · 2 min

The face gets the sunscreen, the serum and the considered evening routine. The neck gets whatever is left on the fingers, and the hands get nothing at all. Then both are held up to the same daylight as the face, several times an hour, for decades. This is about the two places a routine reliably forgets, and why the fix is mostly a matter of extending what you already own rather than buying anything new.

In short

  • Hands take more ultraviolet than anywhere on the body except the face, collect it in moments nobody registers as sun exposure, and are the only skin most of us wash eight or nine times a day.
  • The neck is thinner, carries fewer oil glands, and creases along horizontal bands thousands of times a day. Then there is the plain reason: most people stop applying anything at the jawline.
  • Dedicated neck creams are mostly a packaging decision — take whatever you already use down to the collarbone. Hand cream is the partial exception, and only because washing keeps removing it.
  • Expect gradually more even colour and softer surface texture over months, and only while you keep going. Anything that changes in size, shape or colour, or will not heal, is a question for a doctor.

Why the hands give you away

The skin on the back of the hand is among the thinnest anywhere on the body, and it sits on very little padding. Underneath it, the fat that once smoothed the contour thins with age, so tendons and veins come forward. That part is structural. No cream changes it, and a product implying otherwise is describing something it cannot do. Volume is a clinic's business, not a bathroom shelf's.

What topical care can address is the surface: colour, evenness, texture, and the crepey quality that shows when you make a loose fist. Most of that is sun. Hands take more ultraviolet exposure than anywhere on the body except the face, and unlike the face they collect it in situations nobody registers as sun exposure. The ten minutes to the car. The walk to lunch. An hour with a hand resting on the top of the steering wheel.

Then there is washing. Hands are the only skin most of us cleanse repeatedly through the day, usually with a detergent chosen for hygiene rather than for skin, often in water hotter than necessary, and usually followed by nothing at all. Each wash takes lipids from the outermost layer. The face gets one or two cleanses and a moisturiser afterwards; hands get eight or nine washes and a hot air dryer. Over years that gap shows, and it compounds with everything else in the long arc of how skin ages.

The neck fails for different reasons

The neck has its own set of disadvantages. The skin is thinner than facial skin and carries fewer oil glands, so it holds water less well and shows dryness sooner, as a fine papery texture rather than obvious flaking. It also has less structural scaffolding beneath it than the cheek does.

And it moves, constantly. Every time you turn your head, look down at a phone, swallow, or speak. Facial skin creases in a handful of predictable places; the neck creases along horizontal bands that deepen because they are folded thousands of times a day and then held folded all night, depending on how you sleep and what you sleep on.

The last reason is simpler and slightly embarrassing. Most people stop at the jawline. Sunscreen ends at the jaw. Moisturiser ends at the jaw. After twenty years there is a visible line where the care stopped, and you can see it on people in strong side light: a change in colour and texture at the edge of the mandible. It is close to universal, and it is entirely avoidable.

Almost all of this is sun

You can run the experiment yourself in about four seconds. Turn your arm over and compare the skin on the inner upper arm, which almost never sees daylight, with the skin on the outer forearm. Same person, same age, same genes, same diet, same everything. The difference between those two patches is ultraviolet light and nothing else. On the backs of the hands that difference has had a full lifetime to accumulate with no shade at all.

This is the least ambiguous finding in the whole of skin science, and it is worth stating plainly, because the industry generally prefers to bury it beneath a more interesting ingredient story. The uneven pigmentation, the lost springiness, the fine crosshatching under the knuckles: the dominant cause is cumulative sun, not dehydration, not diet, not the wrong cream.

That conclusion is inconvenient for anyone with something to sell, ourselves included. Prevention outperforms correction here by a margin that is not close. A sunscreen habit kept from thirty onwards does more for how your hands look at sixty than any corrective product begun at fifty-five. Correction is not pointless — appearance can genuinely be improved, and we would not write about routines if it could not — but the gains are modest, slow, and need maintaining. Anyone offering to undo forty years of sun with a jar is selling optimism.

The single highest-value habit

If you take one instruction away, take this one: sunscreen on the backs of the hands, every morning, all year round. It is the highest-value habit available for this part of the body by a wide margin, and hardly anyone does it. It is the same argument as wearing sunscreen every day rather than on holidays, extended twenty centimetres down the arm.

The obstacle is genuine, not imagined: you wash it off. Perfectionism is no use here, but a system is. Keep a tube beside the sink where you actually wash your hands, another in the car door, a small one in whatever bag leaves the house with you. Reapplying at some point after lunch covers the hours that matter most. Sunscreen applied once at eight in the morning is not meaningfully present on your hands by two in the afternoon.

Driving deserves its own mention. Car side glass filters most UVB but lets a good deal of UVA through, and UVA is the wavelength most associated with the visible changes described here. If you commute, your hands sit in direct light on the wheel for hours every week. There is a photograph that circulates in dermatology teaching of a driver who spent decades with one side of his face at the window; the two halves do not look the same age. The same asymmetry turns up on hands, just less dramatically.

Gloves for gardening are sensible. Long sleeves are sensible. But the aim is not to be exhaustive. The aim is to be consistent about the one thing that carries most of the weight.

Extend what you already own

Here is the part that saves money. Dedicated neck creams are, for the most part, a packaging decision. There is nothing in one that a good facial moisturiser does not already contain, and the neck's real requirement is not a special formula but simply being included. Whatever goes on your face should carry on past the jawline to the collarbone. Same products, same evening, in the same order you already apply them, with one extra second of effort.

This is the argument we made about whether the eye area needs its own product, and it holds here too. A separate bottle for a separate patch of skin is usually a business model. The areas that get neglected are neglected because nobody put them on the list, not because they were missing a formulation of their own.

Hands are a partial exception, and only because of the washing. A hand cream earns its place by being reapplied after water has stripped the surface again, not because hand skin needs different chemistry. What you want is something rather dull: humectants to hold water, an occlusive fraction to slow it evaporating away, fragrance-free if you wash often, and substantial enough that it stays where you put it. The elegant one that vanishes in three seconds is doing less than the heavy one you apply at bedtime.

And the direction you rub makes no difference. Upward strokes, downward strokes, lymphatic massage — pleasant, all of it, and none of it changes how the skin looks. Enjoy it if you enjoy it. Do not count it as a step that is working.

What actives can and cannot do here

If you already use a retinoid or an exfoliating acid on your face, both can travel to the neck and hands. Both need more respect than they do on the cheek. Thinner skin with fewer oil glands reacts more readily, and the neck in particular will object at a frequency the face tolerates without comment. Once or twice a week to begin with, moisturiser first if it helps, and an increase only once nothing at all is happening.

Hands are harder again, because washing removes the product and the irritation announces itself as chapping around the knuckles. Applying at night, after the last wash of the day, is the version people actually sustain past a fortnight.

What to expect, honestly: a gradual improvement in how even the skin looks and some softening of surface texture, over months rather than weeks, and only while you keep going. What not to expect: the deeper horizontal bands on the neck to disappear, tendons to be re-covered, or long-standing pigmentation to clear entirely. The honest limit of topical care in these two places is appearance, and appearance improves at the pace of seasons.

One caveat that matters more than any of the above. Marks appear on the hands, face and forearms with age, and most are unremarkable, but these are also the sites where other kinds of skin lesion turn up. Anything that changes in size, shape, colour or texture, or that will not heal, is a question for a doctor rather than a skincare decision. Cosmetics work on how skin looks. Diagnosis is a different profession and should not be attempted from a bathroom mirror.

A short and honest plan

In the morning: sunscreen on the face, then keep going down the neck, then the backs of both hands. Reapply to the hands after washing whenever you remember, and at least once in the afternoon.

In the evening: whatever you use on your face, use to the collarbone. Hand cream last of all, after the final wash, before bed. That is the entire protocol. It costs nothing you are not already spending and adds perhaps ten seconds to a routine you already do.

We have six formulations and none of them is on sale yet, so there is nothing for us to sell you for your hands or your neck. When they are ready, there still will not be. They are made for the face, and the instruction we will give is to take them further down. The most useful thing available for the two areas that show age first is not a product at all. It is a tube of sunscreen kept where you wash your hands, and a couple of decades of picking it up.
