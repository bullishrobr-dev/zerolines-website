The Morning After: Why Alcohol Shows on Your Face

Last updated: 4 January 2027 8 min read Free skin analysis · 2 min

You already know before you look. Something about the way the face sits in the mirror at seven in the morning — softer at the jaw, thicker under the eyes, a pinkness across the cheeks that was not there yesterday. It is not imagination and it is not punishment. Four fairly ordinary pieces of physiology happened overnight, all at once, and your face is where they became visible. Here is what each one does.

In short

  • Four separate things are happening: fluid moves out of circulation into the tissue, surface vessels widen, sleep architecture is disturbed, and the kidneys shed water. Puffy and dehydrated are not the same state.
  • Almost all of it is temporary. Fluid redistributes within a day, colour settles as the vessels return to their usual calibre, and one broken night is repaid by an ordinary one.
  • What helps the next morning is cold, being upright, moving, and water taken with food across the morning rather than in one go. Leave acids and retinoids alone for the day.
  • The disrupted sleep is probably the largest contributor and the one with no remedy the next morning, which may be why it is discussed least.

What you are actually looking at

The word most people reach for is puffy, and it is the right word. What has changed overnight is mostly the soft tissue rather than the skin itself: fluid sitting slightly outside its usual places, so the jawline reads softer, the lids look heavier, and the whole face seems to have gained a millimetre it did not ask for.

Then the colour. A pinkness across the cheeks and the bridge of the nose, sometimes the neck as well, occasionally uneven enough to look blotchy under a bathroom light.

Underneath both, a general flatness. Skin that has had a poor night reflects light less cleanly. Texture reads more than usual, the fine lines around the eyes look drawn rather than soft, and the face looks tired in a way that has almost nothing to do with what you put on it and a great deal to do with what happened while you were asleep.

Four things are running at once here, and they are worth separating, because they are not equally important and they do not respond to the same remedies.

Fluid does not disappear, it relocates

Alcohol suppresses vasopressin, the hormone that tells the kidneys to hold on to water. Less vasopressin, more urine, and by the time you go to bed you are running a fluid deficit. That is the part everyone knows.

But puffiness and dehydration are not the same thing. If anything they look like opposites, which is why the standard advice can feel confusing at seven in the morning when you are simultaneously parched and swollen. What produces the swelling is not how much fluid you have lost but where the remaining fluid has gone. Alcohol provokes a low-grade inflammatory response, small vessels become a little leakier, and fluid moves out of circulation into the spaces between cells. Salt eaten late — the crisps, the olives, whatever was on the table at one in the morning — holds fluid with it. And then you spend six or seven hours horizontal, during which gravity stops draining the face and starts filling it.

That last point explains the timing. Puffiness peaks on waking and eases across the first hour or two upright, because standing up is itself a drainage mechanism.

The area under the eyes shows it first and worst. The skin there is the thinnest on the body, it is loosely attached to what lies beneath, and there is very little structural padding to absorb a small volume of fluid — which is the reason the eye area behaves differently from the rest of the face in almost every context, not just this one. A quantity of fluid that would be invisible on your cheek is plainly visible on your lower lid.

Why the colour shows up first

Alcohol widens blood vessels. Those near the surface of the skin carry more blood, closer to the surface, and the result is warmth and colour — usually across the cheeks, the nose and the upper chest. In most people it appears within a drink or two and fades within hours of the last one. It is a circulatory event rather than a skin event, which is why nothing applied topically has much bearing on it.

A subset of people flush hard and immediately, sometimes after half a glass. This is generally understood to come down to acetaldehyde, the compound alcohol is broken into before it is cleared, and to a common inherited variation in one of the enzymes responsible for clearing it. The variant is widespread among people of East Asian descent. It is a matter of genetics rather than tolerance, habit or anything a person is doing wrong.

Repeated, heavy flushing over many years is associated with small surface vessels that stay visible once the evening is over. If facial redness has stopped being an occasional event and become a permanent feature of your face, that is worth a conversation with a doctor rather than a change of moisturiser. There are causes with names and with medical treatments, and skincare is not the right tool for any of them.

The sleep you think you had

This is the mechanism that gets the least attention and probably deserves the most.

Alcohol is sedating. You fall asleep faster after drinking, and that is precisely why it has a reputation as a sleep aid. But falling asleep quickly and sleeping well are different achievements. What alcohol does to the architecture of the night is fairly consistent in the sleep literature: it suppresses REM sleep in the first half, and then, as the body finishes metabolising it, the sedative effect withdraws and the second half of the night becomes shallow and broken. You surface repeatedly, often without remembering it. Body temperature is disturbed. There are more trips to the bathroom, for the reasons above.

The consequence for your face is that you have spent a night in the wrong stages of sleep. The deeper phases are when most of the skin's overnight repair work happens — blood flow to the skin rises, cellular turnover accelerates, and the body's own maintenance runs at its highest rate of the day. Fragment those hours and you wake having had the duration without the benefit.

If you have ever noticed that seven hours after drinking leaves you looking worse than five hours of ordinary sleep, this is why. It is not the volume of sleep that changed. It is the shape of it.

What is temporary and what accumulates

Almost all of it is temporary. The fluid redistributes within a day, usually less. The colour settles once the vessels return to their normal calibre. One disrupted night of sleep is repaid, more or less, by an ordinary one. If you drank on Saturday and your face looks like itself again by Monday, nothing lasting has happened, whatever the mirror suggested on Sunday.

What accumulates is a slower and less dramatic story. Sustained heavy drinking over years is associated with a face that looks older than its owner: more visible surface vessels, thinner and less resilient-looking skin, a duller and less even tone. Chronic short sleep contributes independently. None of this arrives after a weekend, and none of it is well described by a number.

The honest limit of this is that alcohol is very difficult to isolate. It tends to travel with other things — later nights, more sun the following day, more smoking, worse eating, more stress — and studies that try to separate them are working with self-reported intake, which is not a reliable measurement. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that alcohol belongs on the list of the everyday habits that show up in a face over decades, somewhere below smoking and sun exposure. Where exactly a glass of wine with dinner sits on that list is genuinely unsettled, and the evidence is thinner than the confidence with which it is usually stated in either direction.

What actually helps the next morning

Nothing here is dramatic, and anything that promises to be should be treated with suspicion.

Cold helps, briefly and mechanically. Cold water on the face for half a minute, or a cold flannel held against the lids, narrows surface vessels and reduces the look of swelling for a while. Sleeping on an extra pillow works better than anything you can do the next morning, if you have the presence of mind for it the night before.

Being upright helps more than any product. So does moving — a walk, stairs, anything that gets circulation going. Gentle facial massage in the direction of the lymph nodes does genuinely move fluid, and the effect is real, modest and short-lived. It is a way of speeding up something that was going to happen anyway. It is not doing anything structural, and anyone telling you otherwise is overselling a stone.

Drink water, with something to eat, over the course of the morning rather than a litre in one go. This corrects a genuine shortfall, which is the one situation in which water intake visibly affects how skin looks. Go easy on salt for the day.

And leave your skin alone. A morning after is not the time for acids, retinoids or vigorous exfoliation. The barrier is not at its best, the skin is more reactive than usual, and the temptation to correct the face aggressively tends to produce a second problem on top of the first. Cleanse gently, moisturise, wear sunscreen, get on with the day.

The part that gets least attention

Almost all the advice on this subject fixes on dehydration, because dehydration has an obvious and satisfying remedy. Drink the water. Feel virtuous.

The sleep disruption is probably the bigger contributor, and it has no remedy the following morning, which may be exactly why it is discussed less. You cannot drink your way back into slow-wave sleep you did not get. If you want to protect how your face looks on a Sunday, the effective levers are the ones that protect the night: finishing earlier, eating properly alongside, and giving yourself a real night's sleep afterwards.

This is not a lecture and it is not an argument for abstinence. It is a description of a mechanism, and you are entitled to weigh a good evening against a soft-looking morning however you like. Most people, most of the time, decide the evening was worth it.

What we would rather not do is pretend a formulation solves it. Skincare works at the surface, on appearance, over weeks. It has nothing to say to a night of fragmented sleep, and any product suggesting otherwise is describing a wish rather than a mechanism. The morning after is a sleep problem wearing a skincare costume.
