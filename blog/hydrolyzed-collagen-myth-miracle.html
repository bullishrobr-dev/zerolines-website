Hydrolysed Collagen in Skincare: Myth or Miracle?

Last updated: 13 May 2026 7 min read

The skincare world is split on collagen. One camp insists the molecules are simply too big to cross the skin barrier, which would make topical collagen pointless. The other camp points to before-and-after photos of firmer, smoother-looking skin. They can't both be right. Or can they?

The answer depends on what you mean by "collagen" — and on what you expect it to do.

The Molecular Weight Problem

Native collagen — the structural protein found in skin, bones, and connective tissue — has a molecular weight of approximately 300,000 daltons. The skin's barrier, the stratum corneum, blocks molecules larger than about 500 daltons. By that measure, native collagen has no chance of penetrating.

But hydrolysed collagen is different. The hydrolysis process breaks the large protein chains into smaller peptides and amino acids. Depending on the degree of hydrolysis, these fragments can range from 1,000 daltons down to under 300 daltons — small enough to pass through the barrier.

The key variable is the degree of hydrolysis. Not all "hydrolysed collagen" is the same. A loosely hydrolysed product might contain peptides of 5,000–10,000 daltons that won't penetrate. A more aggressively hydrolysed product might contain peptides under 500 daltons that can reach the dermis.

At Zero Lines, we use collagen hydrolysed to an average molecular weight of 3,000 daltons — a deliberate compromise. Smaller peptides penetrate deeper but are less stable. Larger peptides are more stable but sit on the surface. Our target size balances penetration with structural integrity.

What Happens When It Gets Through?

This is the part most brands skip over. Even when collagen peptides do get through, they don't slot into the skin's existing collagen. That was never the point. They work as signalling molecules instead, nudging fibroblasts into thinking nearby collagen has been damaged and needs repairing.

Scientists call this the "matrikine" effect. Collagen-derived peptides mimic the body's natural wound-healing signals, stimulating fibroblasts to produce new collagen, elastin, and hyaluronic acid. The peptides themselves don't become structural collagen; they trigger the cells that do.

The chain of events is well understood. Collagen-derived peptides look, to the skin, like the debris left behind when tissue is damaged, so fibroblasts read them as a cue to rebuild. Fragments under roughly 1,000 daltons can reach the dermis, which is exactly where that conversation needs to happen. What shows up at the surface is skin that looks firmer and better hydrated, with fine lines that have less to settle into. The effect is gradual, and it is signalling rather than scaffolding.

Oral vs. Topical: Which Works Better?

Both routes appear effective, through different mechanisms:

Topical hydrolysed collagen acts directly on the skin where applied. It provides immediate surface hydration (collagen peptides are humectants) and longer-term signalling effects. The limitation: it only affects the areas where it's applied.

Oral hydrolysed collagen is absorbed into the bloodstream as di- and tri-peptides, then carried around the body. Those peptides gather in skin tissue and keep fibroblasts active systemically, not just where a cream happens to land. The trade-off: it can take eight to twelve weeks before anything is visible.

Our view: both have value. The Precision Collagen Activation Syringe delivers topical peptides for targeted, immediate action. For those who want systemic support, we recommend adding a high-quality oral collagen supplement — though we don't currently produce one ourselves.

The Source Matters

Collagen can be derived from bovine, porcine, marine, or avian sources. Each has a different amino acid profile:

  • Bovine: High in type I and III collagen, closely matching human skin collagen. Most extensively studied.
  • Marine (fish): Smaller peptide size due to natural structure, potentially better absorption. Rich in type I.
  • Porcine: Structurally similar to human collagen but avoided by some for religious or personal reasons.

We use bovine-derived hydrolysed collagen in our formulations — specifically type I collagen from grass-fed sources — because its amino acid profile most closely matches the collagen in human skin, and because the research base is strongest. One point worth clearing up: marine collagen is not the same thing as the brown and red algae you find in marine skincare, which do their work through entirely different compounds.

Does It Actually Work?

The honest answer: yes, with caveats. Hydrolysed collagen is no miracle worker that smooths deep lines overnight. Used consistently, at a sensible concentration, it is a well-supported ingredient that improves hydration and elasticity and visibly softens fine lines over eight to twelve weeks.

The key factors that determine effectiveness:

  1. Molecular weight: Must be low enough to penetrate. Look for products that specify peptide size or degree of hydrolysis.
  2. Concentration: Effective products typically contain 2–5% hydrolysed collagen. Below 1%, the signalling effect is minimal.
  3. Formulation pH: Collagen peptides are most stable at pH 5–7. Extreme acidity or alkalinity degrades them.
  4. Consistency: like most repair ingredients, collagen peptides need eight to twelve weeks of daily use before the difference shows, and where they sit in your routine affects how much of that difference you get.

The Molecular Size Problem

Collagen is a large protein — a triple helix of amino acids with a molecular weight around 300,000 Daltons. Skin is designed to keep things out, not let them in. The stratum corneum, the skin's outermost layer, has a pore size of approximately 50–100 Daltons. A collagen molecule is roughly 3,000–6,000 times too large to penetrate.

Hydrolysis breaks collagen into smaller peptide fragments. "Hydrolysed collagen" in skincare typically contains fragments ranging from 1,000 to 10,000 Daltons. This is smaller than intact collagen, but still 10–200 times larger than what can penetrate the stratum corneum. Some manufacturers claim their hydrolysed collagen is "micronised" or "nano-sized" — but even at 500 Daltons, penetration is minimal and unreliable.

What Actually Works for Collagen

If hydrolysed collagen can't get in, what can? Signal peptides can. These short chains of three to five amino acids slip through the barrier and prompt fibroblasts to build collagen from within. Vitamin C matters too; the body can't cross-link or stabilise collagen without it. Retinoids push production up by acting on fibroblast gene expression, and niacinamide shores up the barrier those cells depend on. Then there is the quiet one: daily UV protection, which stops the collagen you already have from breaking down. That single habit does much of the heavy lifting, since collagen loss is one of the main reasons skin changes after 30.

Our Collagen Syringe uses signal peptides rather than hydrolysed collagen. These peptides penetrate the stratum corneum and communicate with fibroblasts, triggering genuine collagen synthesis rather than merely applying a protein that sits on the surface.
