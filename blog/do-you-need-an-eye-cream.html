Do You Need an Eye Cream?

Last updated: 2 November 2026 7 min read Free skin analysis · 2 min

The honest answer is sometimes, and less often than you are told. The skin around the eye is genuinely different from the skin on your cheek, in ways that are easy to describe and easier to overstate. Whether that difference requires a product of its own has less to do with anatomy than with two practical questions: what you can tolerate there, and how your moisturiser behaves once it starts to move. Here is the narrower true version.

In short

  • Only two reasons a separate eye formulation genuinely exists: tolerance — the same active at a lower strength, built without the things that sting in a tear film — and texture, light enough to stay where you put it.
  • If your moisturiser is fragrance-free, not heavily active, low in volatile alcohol and light enough not to travel, take it up to the orbital bone and you already have an eye cream.
  • Dark circles have three separate causes: pigment, vessels showing through translucent skin, and shadow cast by bone and fat. Only the first answers to anything topical, and slowly.
  • The two things that do most for this area over a decade are sunglasses, because squinting is a mechanical habit, and a sunscreen taken right up to the orbital bone rather than stopping short of it.

The skin there really is different

Start with the part that is true. The skin of the eyelid is the thinnest on the body — thin enough that it tends to be the example anatomists reach for when they need one. There is very little fat beneath it, and under that sits a ring of muscle you use every time you blink, which is thousands of times a day, almost none of them noticed.

It also has far fewer oil glands than the skin a couple of centimetres away on your cheek. Less of that oily film means less of the buffer that slows water leaving and irritants arriving. Which is why the area shows dehydration first. A short night, a long flight, a dry hotel room, and the crinkling at the outer corner appears before anything else on your face has changed at all.

And it is more translucent than the skin around it. What reads as a shadow under the eye is often not pigment on the surface but the blue-grey of vessels underneath, seen through skin too thin to hide them. That is anatomy rather than a stain, and nothing applied on top of it will lift it.

What that difference does and does not imply

The step from this skin is different to this skin needs its own product is where the argument usually gives way. The difference is real and measurable. The conclusion drawn from it is where the selling happens.

Thin, here, does not mean delicate in the way advertising uses the word. It means the threshold is lower. Reactions show sooner, dryness shows sooner, and anything that stings will sting more. That is a good reason to be careful about what you put there. It is not, on its own, a reason to buy a second jar.

There are two reasons a separate eye formulation genuinely exists. Neither is exciting. One is tolerance, and one is texture. Almost everything else the category says about eye creams is an elaboration built on top of those two.

The first real reason is tolerance

Actives behave differently at the orbital rim. A retinoid at a strength your cheeks accept without comment can leave the eye area tight, flaking and pink for a fortnight. The same is true of strong exfoliating acids, and of vitamin C at the high end. Nothing has gone wrong when that happens. The same dose has simply landed on skin with less capacity to absorb the hit.

So an eye version of an active is usually the same molecule at a lower strength, often buffered, often in a base designed to slow how quickly it gets going. That is a sensible piece of formulation and it is worth paying for, if you use those actives and want them working near your eyes. If you don't use them, you are buying a solution to a problem you do not have.

Tolerance has a second half, which is what happens when a product migrates into the eye itself. Fragrance, essential oils and a heavy load of volatile alcohol are unremarkable on a cheek and genuinely unpleasant in a tear film. Formulations intended for the eye area are normally tested for that contact and built without the ingredients most likely to sting. It is worth being precise about what that testing means, though: it is a comfort and safety question, not a performance claim.

The second real reason is texture

Put a rich cream on the orbital bone and it will not stay on the orbital bone. Body heat thins it, blinking moves it, and within an hour some proportion of it has crept towards the lash line. Occasionally that means blurred vision for a few minutes. More often it just means the area looks slick and the concealer breaks up over it by eleven.

Eye formulations tend to be lighter-bodied for exactly this reason: enough emollience to keep the skin comfortable, not so much that it travels. That matters more than it sounds like it should. Most complaints about eye creams — the stinging, the watering, the smudging, the greasy look — are texture problems rather than ingredient problems, and a lighter texture solves them without any clever chemistry at all.

Some people also find that rich textures around the eye leave small firm bumps behind. Whether the cream caused them is harder to establish than the internet suggests. If they persist, they are worth showing to a doctor rather than treating with another product.

When your moisturiser is already an eye cream

Here is the part the category would rather not spell out. If your moisturiser is fragrance-free, not heavily active, low in volatile alcohol and light enough to stay where you put it, you can take it up to the orbital bone and you have an eye cream. The same humectants, the same barrier lipids, the same everything that actually does the work.

The bone is the boundary. Apply to the orbital rim, the ridge you can feel with a fingertip, and not to the mobile lid or along the lash line. Product travels inwards on its own; start close and it finishes in your eye. A small amount, patted rather than dragged, is plenty — the skin there does not need much and does not appreciate being pulled.

Where it sits in the sequence matters roughly as much as what it is: thinnest textures first, richest last, with the eye application going on before anything occlusive covers it.

Whether you need it twice a day is a fair question. Morning and evening are doing quite different jobs, and a good number of people only want something around the eye at night, when nothing has to sit under make-up and a slightly richer texture can be forgiven for wandering.

What people actually want from it

Ask people why they want an eye cream and the answers cluster into three: dark circles, puffiness, and the fine lines at the outer corner. Worth taking them one at a time, because what a topical can honestly offer varies enormously across the three, and the marketing tends to flatten that difference.

Dark circles have several distinct causes and they do not share a solution. Some of it is pigment in the skin. Some of it is vessels showing through. Some of it is pure shadow, cast by the hollow between the lower lid and the cheek — a matter of bone and fat position, largely inherited, and entirely unmoved by anything in a jar. A brightening formulation may, over months, make the pigment kind look a little less obvious. It will do nothing whatever to the other two. Working out which sort you have is more useful than any product decision you will make.

Puffiness is mostly fluid, and fluid answers to sleep, position, salt and what you drank the evening before far more than it answers to skincare. Caffeine in a formulation constricts vessels briefly and can make the area look tighter for an hour or two, which is a real change in appearance and a short one. Moving fluid around by hand does something comparable and lasts about as long. Both are fine. Neither is a correction. And if swelling is sudden, one-sided, or has not settled by mid-morning, that is a question for a doctor rather than for a cream.

The fine lines are where a topical does its best work, and even that needs a caveat. Well-hydrated skin at the outer corner looks smoother within hours, because the crinkling of a dehydrated surface really is a hydration effect and really does resolve. A crease still visible when your face is completely at rest is a different proposition, and nothing you can buy over a counter removes it. Softening how it looks is achievable. Erasing it is not, and any product that says otherwise is promising something it cannot support.

So do you need one

Probably not, if your moisturiser is bland, light and doesn't sting when it drifts. Take it up to the bone and put the money somewhere it will do more.

Probably yes, if one of these is true. You use retinoids or acids and want them near the eyes at a strength suited to the area. Your moisturiser is rich, fragranced, or high in volatile alcohol. Or it migrates, and you are tired of blinking through it. Those are the honest cases, and they are specific enough to check in about a minute in front of a mirror.

Then the unromantic part. The two things that do most for how this area looks over a decade are not creams at all: sunglasses, because squinting is a mechanical habit and the skin there keeps the record of it, and a sunscreen you will genuinely apply right up to the orbital bone rather than stopping short of it. Neither is interesting. Both outperform the jar.
