Does Skin Get Used to a Product? The Tolerance Question

Last updated: 14 December 2026 7 min read Free skin analysis · 2 min

It usually arrives around week ten. The cream that was doing something has stopped doing it, the reflection looks the way it looked before, and the obvious explanation presents itself: skin gets used to things. It is one of the most widely held beliefs in skincare, and it is mostly wrong. But it is wrong in an interesting way, and the thing that actually happened is more useful to know than the thing people assume.

In short

  • Skin does not habituate to a moisturiser the way a body habituates to caffeine. Glycerin holds water because it is hygroscopic and petrolatum slows loss because it is a physical barrier. Neither has a receptor to downregulate.
  • Three ordinary explanations account for nearly every case: the first fix was the biggest fix, your eye recalibrated around the result, and the season changed.
  • Retinoids are the genuine exception, and it is usually described backwards. What fades with continued use is the irritation, not the effect — skin becomes less reactive, not less responsive.
  • Good reasons to change: it stings, the texture is wrong for the season, or a fair run checked against a photograph showed nothing. Boredom counts too, provided you call it boredom.

What people are actually describing

The complaint has a consistent shape, which is the first clue that something ordinary is going on. Weeks one to four are good. Visibly good, the kind of good where someone at work asks whether you have been away. Weeks five to eight are quieter. By week twelve the mirror looks roughly the way it always looked, and the conclusion arrives on its own: the product has stopped working, my skin got used to it.

The word people reach for is tolerance, borrowed from caffeine and alcohol and the general sense that a body learns to ignore anything given to it often enough. The implied model is skin as an audience that gets bored, receptors that stop listening, a system that shrugs. The remedy follows neatly from the model. Rotate. Change the cream every few months. Keep the skin guessing.

It is a tidy theory, and in almost every case people apply it to, it is not what happened. Three unremarkable things account for nearly all of it, and none of them involves skin learning anything.

What tolerance means when it means something

Tolerance is a specific thing rather than a general mood. In pharmacology it means that repeated exposure to a substance produces a progressively smaller response, and it works through mechanisms that can be described: receptors reduce in number or sensitivity, the enzymes that clear the substance become more efficient, feedback loops reset around the new normal. Caffeine is the familiar case. Regular drinkers develop more adenosine receptors, so the same cup blocks a proportionally smaller share of the signal it used to block. That is real, measurable, and it reverses when you stop.

Now ask what the equivalent would be for glycerin. Glycerin holds water in the outer layer of skin because it is hygroscopic, meaning it attracts water molecules the way salt left out in a Barcelona August goes damp. That is a property of the molecule, not a negotiation with a cell. There is no receptor to downregulate. Petrolatum slows water leaving the skin because it is a physical barrier sitting on top of it. Physics does not get bored.

The honest limit of this is that skin is living tissue, it adapts to plenty, and a few skincare ingredients genuinely do act on receptors. A flat nothing ever adapts would be its own overclaim. But the everyday version of the belief, that a moisturiser stops moisturising because you have used it too long, has no mechanism behind it that anyone has been able to set out. It is a folk theory filling a gap where an explanation was wanted.

The first fix was the biggest fix

Here is the first of the three ordinary explanations, and on its own it accounts for most of the cases. The dramatic early improvement was not the product working at full strength and then tiring. It was a deficit being corrected.

If you begin with skin that is dry at the surface, roughened by too much washing and underprotected from sun, then almost any competent moisturiser plus a sunscreen will produce a visible change within a fortnight. Surface water content rises. Light sits differently on the cheek. Lines that were partly dehydration look softer, because they were partly dehydration. That change is genuine and worth having. It is also finite. Once the deficit is filled there is no more deficit to fill.

The product has not stopped working. The work has changed character, from repair to maintenance, and maintenance looks exactly like nothing happening. A roof that has stopped leaking is not a roof that has stopped being a roof. This is why the first thirty days of a new routine are such a poor guide to the next three hundred. The curve is steep and then it flattens, and the flattening is the design rather than the failure.

Your eye moves the goalposts

The second explanation is that you are not comparing what you think you are comparing. You are comparing today's face to yesterday's, and yesterday's to the day before, in an unbroken chain in which nothing ever looks like much of a change. An improvement is absorbed into the baseline within about a week. After that it is simply what your skin looks like, and what your skin looks like is never quite enough.

Perceptual recalibration is one of the better-established findings about human judgement, and it is not a failure of attention. It is how the visual system stays useful. The consequence for skincare is that self-assessment across long stretches is close to worthless without a fixed reference. Almost nobody takes the photograph. The few who do, same window, same hour, no expression, are frequently surprised, and in both directions.

The same machinery runs the other way across years, which is why the slow arithmetic of skin ageing stays invisible while it is happening. Nobody watches themselves age in a mirror. They notice it in a photograph from four years ago, all at once, and it feels like something that happened last Tuesday.

The season changed

The third explanation is the dullest and probably the most common. The cream did not change. The air did.

Cold air holds less water, and indoor heating dries a room further. Water leaves skin faster in January than in September, so a moisturiser that felt exactly right in autumn can feel thin by midwinter while doing precisely what it always did. Air conditioning produces the same effect in reverse in August, which is a specific problem in Marbella and a general one anywhere the machine runs all day. Add a few flights, a move to harder water, a stretch of poor sleep, and the picture stops being mysterious.

So before concluding that a product has failed, it is worth asking what else moved. Something usually did. A rich cream that seems to have stopped earning its place in June is often just a rich cream, in June.

Where something like tolerance is real

There is one part of a routine where a genuine adaptation happens, and it is worth being precise about it, because it is regularly described backwards.

Retinoids irritate at first. Dryness, flaking, tightness, a stinging response to products that never stung before. This tends to show up in the early weeks, varies a great deal between people, and for some does not appear at all. With continued regular use the reaction typically settles. The skin has adapted to the ingredient. This is the closest thing to tolerance in the whole field, and it is sometimes called retinisation.

The distinction that matters is this: what fades is the irritation, not the effect. The skin becomes less reactive, not less responsive. You are not getting less out of it, you are paying less for it. That is close to the opposite of what tolerance means in pharmacology, and it is why introducing a retinoid slowly enough to keep the irritation manageable is most of the skill involved.

A cousin of this confusion attaches to acids. People read the tingle as the working, so when the tingle fades they assume the acid has given up, and they raise the frequency to get the sensation back. Sensation is a poor proxy for almost anything happening in skin. It is one of the reasons exfoliating weekly rather than daily tends to end better than the alternative.

What rotating costs you

If skin genuinely habituated, rotating products would be sound practice. It mostly does not, so rotation is mostly a cost, and the costs compound.

The first is money, which is obvious enough. The second is time: most formulations want something like eight to twelve weeks before a fair judgement is possible, and a routine that turns over every six weeks never produces a verdict on anything. The third is attribution. Change one thing and you learn something. Change four and all you have learned is that four things, together, did or did not do something, which is not knowledge you can use next time.

There is a quieter fourth cost. Every new product is a new set of unknowns, and people whose skin seems to react to everything are very often people who have been changing everything for a year. Some of the impulse to rotate comes from the belief that certain ingredients must be kept apart on alternate nights, a caution more often repeated than demonstrated. Whether niacinamide and vitamin C belong in the same routine is a fair example of a rule that has outgrown the evidence for it.

We are an odd party to be making this argument. Zero Lines has six formulations and none of them are on sale, so there is nothing here for you to rotate onto.

None of which means never change anything. If a product stings consistently, change it. If the texture is wrong for the season, change it. If you have given it a fair run, a season rather than a fortnight, with a photograph to check against, and nothing has moved, change it. Those are reasons. Boredom is a reason too, provided you call it boredom. What is worth resisting is the idea that a change is owed, that the skin has quietly stopped listening and needs to be surprised. It has not. Most of the time the product finished the job it was good at, your eye adjusted around the result, and then the weather turned.

Sources & References

  • It is generally understood in pharmacology that tolerance means a smaller response to the same dose after repeated exposure, arising from describable changes such as receptors adjusting in number or sensitivity. Caffeine and adenosine receptors are the standard teaching example. No comparable mechanism has been described for a topical humectant or occlusive.
  • It is well established that skin loses water to the air faster in cold, dry conditions and in low indoor humidity, and that this is measurable rather than impressionistic. Seasonal change in how a moisturiser performs is expected on this basis alone.
  • The tendency of retinoid-associated dryness and stinging to settle with continued regular use is a long-standing and widely reported clinical observation. It is generally described as the skin becoming less reactive to the ingredient, not as the ingredient becoming less active.
  • Perceptual adaptation, meaning that a judgement about something shifts according to what one has recently been looking at, is among the more robust general findings in the psychology of perception, and it is not specific to skin.
