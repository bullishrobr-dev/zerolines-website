Air Conditioning, Central Heating, and the Air Your Skin Actually Lives In

Last updated: 7 December 2026 8 min read Free skin analysis · 2 min

You know the weather where you live. You almost certainly do not know the humidity of the room you are sitting in, and that room is where your skin spends nearly all of its life. Office, car, bedroom, aircraft cabin: all of it air that a machine has either cooled or warmed, and both of those processes leave it drier than they found it. The effect on how skin looks is real. It is also slower, and duller, than the internet would like.

In short

  • Cooling and heating both end up drying: an air conditioner condenses water out of the air, and central heating adds none while raising the temperature, so the same water reads as a far lower relative humidity.
  • The damage is cumulative rather than dramatic — tightness for twenty minutes after cleansing, a papery look at seven in the morning, fine lines reading more clearly in February than in June.
  • A hygrometer and then a humidifier in the bedroom will do more than anything you can apply. Somewhere around forty to fifty per cent suits most rooms; above sixty brings condensation and mould, which is the worse problem.
  • A cream narrows the gap without closing it. Occlusives applied to damp skin, weighted towards the evening, are the part doing the work — and drinking more water does not change the humidity of the room.

The climate that matters is the one indoors

Ask someone how their environment affects their skin and they will describe the city. Damp Atlantic winters, dry alpine air, the salt that comes off a Mediterranean coast. All of that is true, and we have written about how the climate outside your window changes what your skin needs. But look at the hours. Most people in northern Europe spend the overwhelming majority of their time indoors or in a vehicle. The outdoor climate is largely something you commute through.

Indoors is a manufactured climate, and it is manufactured along exactly one axis. Your thermostat has a temperature dial. It does not have a humidity dial. Whatever the humidity ends up being is a side effect of the temperature somebody asked for, and in both directions that side effect points the same way: down.

Almost nobody has seen a number for the air in their own bedroom. A hygrometer costs about what a bottle of shampoo costs, and it is probably the most informative thing you can buy for your skin this year, which is not a sentence anyone in this industry enjoys writing.

Why cooling and heating both end up drying

Air conditioning does not simply chill air. It draws air across a coil cold enough that the water vapour in it condenses out, delivers the cooled, drier air back to the room, and drains the water away. The puddle under an outdoor unit, the drip down a wall in a Spanish street in August, is the humidity of somebody's room going somewhere else. An air conditioner is a dehumidifier that also happens to make you comfortable.

Central heating works the other way round and arrives in the same place. It adds no water. But warm air holds far more water vapour than cold air, so the same quantity of water in a warmer room is a much lower relative humidity. Take January air from outside, near saturation at three degrees, bring it in and heat it to twenty-one, and you have air that contains exactly what it did before and behaves like a desert. The driest air most northern Europeans meet all year is indoors in February.

Relative humidity is the number that matters to skin, because evaporation follows a gradient. The surface of your skin is wet in a way that the air is not, and water moves from the wetter place towards the drier one at a rate set by the size of that difference. Dry air does not attack anything. It simply widens the gradient, and physics does the rest, continuously, without drama.

The four rooms worth thinking about

Aeroplanes are the extreme case and the one everybody notices. Cabin air is drawn from outside at altitude, where there is almost no water vapour, and while it is pressurised and warmed, it is not humidified. Long-haul cabins generally sit around ten to twenty per cent relative humidity, drier than most deserts. Nine hours of that is not imaginary. It is why your face looks flat and your eyes gritty on landing.

Offices are less obvious and add up to more, because you are in one five days a week. Large building systems are tuned for temperature and fresh air; humidity is whatever falls out of that. If your desk sits under a ceiling diffuser you spend the working day in a slow cool draught. Winter offices are frequently drier than summer ones, which surprises people who blame the air conditioning and never think about the boiler.

Cars are small, sealed, and blowing conditioned air directly at your face at head height. The demist setting is worse: on most cars it runs the compressor deliberately, because dry air clears a windscreen fastest. Point the vents at the glass or the footwell rather than at yourself, which costs nothing.

Then the bedroom, which is the significant one, because it is eight uninterrupted hours in the same air with the window shut and a radiator under it. It also coincides with the hours in which skin does most of its repair work. If you wake with your face feeling tight before you have washed it, and a pillow crease that takes longer to settle than it used to, the bedroom air is a reasonable suspect.

Cumulative rather than dramatic

This is where the honest version parts company with the marketing one. Dry air does not injure skin the way an afternoon of unprotected sun does. There is no single exposure that matters much. What there is instead is a small persistent deficit, the surface losing water slightly faster than it is replaced, month after month, and the visible consequences are a matter of accumulation.

What that looks like is unremarkable and familiar. Skin that feels tight for twenty minutes after cleansing. A slightly papery, matte quality in the bathroom mirror at seven in the morning. Fine lines that read more clearly in February than in June, not because they have deepened but because a well-hydrated surface reflects light more evenly than a dehydrated one. Make-up that sits on top instead of settling in. These are appearance changes, and they are largely reversible when the air improves.

Attribution is genuinely messy, and we would rather say so. Winter moves several variables at once. Showers get longer and hotter. Hands get washed more often. The mineral load of your tap water does its own quiet work on the skin barrier regardless of the season. Less daylight, worse sleep, more heating. Nobody can tell you what share of your February skin is the air, and anyone who offers you a percentage has made it up.

The unglamorous answer is a humidifier

The most effective thing you can do about dry indoor air is put water back into it. Not onto your face, into the room. A humidifier is a plastic box that hums in the corner and needs refilling, and for this problem it is comfortably more effective than anything you could apply to your skin. We are aware this is not what anyone wants to hear from a skincare house. It remains true.

Buy the hygrometer first and watch it for a week before changing anything. Somewhere around forty to fifty per cent suits most people and most buildings. Below thirty you will feel it in your throat as much as your face. Above sixty you start to get condensation on cold window reveals and the outside walls of older flats, and mould is a considerably worse problem than tight skin.

Put it in the bedroom before anywhere else. Eight consecutive hours is the longest stretch of air you genuinely control, so it is the cheapest place to win. Clean it often. An unwashed reservoir distributes whatever has been growing in it around the room you sleep in, and a badly maintained humidifier is worse than none.

Cheaper adjustments, roughly in order of usefulness: turn the heating down a degree, because cooler air raises relative humidity for the same amount of water; dry laundry on an airer indoors, which is slightly grim and genuinely effective; leave the bathroom door open after a shower; keep the car vents off your face. And one thing that does nothing here, however often it is repeated — drinking more water does not change the humidity of the room. It matters for its own reasons. It does not alter the gradient your face is losing water across.

What a cream can and cannot do against the air

Topical skincare is not useless here. It is working against a much larger system. The useful category is occlusives: heavier film-forming materials such as petrolatum, waxes, plant butters and skin-identical lipids, which sit on the surface and slow the rate at which water leaves it. This is old, well-understood, deeply unglamorous technology, and in dry air it is the part of a moisturiser doing most of the work.

Humectants, glycerin and urea and hyaluronic acid among them, hold water in the surface layers, and they are excellent when there is water available to hold. In very dry air their behaviour is more complicated, which is the reason hyaluronic acid needs something sealing it in when the room is dry. A humectant with nothing over it, in a heated office in January, is doing half a job.

The mechanical advice is dull and it works. Apply to skin still damp, within a minute of washing, so the occlusive traps water already there rather than asking it to find some. Weight the routine towards the evening, because the overnight exposure is the longest one.

The honest limit is this. A moisturiser is a few micrometres of material standing between your face and eight hours of moving, mechanically dried air. It narrows the gap. It does not close it, and no formulation — ours included, when there is one — fully compensates for the air it is fighting. If skin cracks, weeps, or itches enough to wake you, that has gone past dry air and a cream, and belongs with a doctor.

If you change one thing

Measure first. It costs almost nothing, it takes a week, and it turns a vague seasonal complaint into a number you can act on. Most people are startled by how low their bedroom reads in January and how low their office reads all year round.

Then fix the bedroom, because that is where the hours are. Then adjust what you put on your face — heavier at night through the winter, lighter when the air outside is humid again in June — rather than buying a new routine every time the season turns.

The industry sells this as a problem a cream solves. It is closer to a building services problem that a cream can partially mitigate. We would rather you knew which of the two you were paying for.
