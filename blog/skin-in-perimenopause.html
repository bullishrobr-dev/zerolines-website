Skin in Perimenopause: What Changes, and What Skincare Can Honestly Do

Last updated: 11 January 2027 8 min read Free skin analysis · 4 min

Somewhere in her forties, often sooner than she expected, a woman notices her face has changed in a way that moisturiser does not fix. Not a new line. A quality. The skin sits differently on the bone. What follows is what is understood about that change, what skincare can honestly do about how it looks, and the larger part that is not a skincare question at all.

The transition, described plainly

Perimenopause is the stretch of years before periods stop for good. It commonly begins in the forties, sometimes the late thirties, and can last two years or ten. The word attached to it is usually decline, which is not quite right. Oestrogen does not fall in a tidy line. It swings, sometimes to peaks higher than anything in the preceding decade, then drops away. The instability is the defining feature, not the eventual low.

Skin is one of the tissues paying attention to all this. Oestrogen receptors sit in the fibroblasts of the dermis, in the keratinocytes of the surface, in sebaceous glands and hair follicles. Oestrogen is bound up with how much collagen is made, how much water the skin holds, how much oil it produces and how quickly it repairs itself. When the signal changes, the tissue changes with it. That is not a fault in your skin. It is your skin working exactly as it is built to.

One thing is worth saying plainly, so it need not be repeated below. This is a physiological transition, not a skincare problem. Everything described here concerns how skin looks and feels. Anything to do with the transition itself, its timing, its other effects on the body, or what to do about it medically, belongs in a conversation with a doctor who knows your history. A journal article is not that conversation, and neither is a cream.

The collagen story, and how much of it we actually know

A figure circulates in this subject. You will have seen it, phrased with confidence, about how much of the skin's collagen disappears in the first years after menopause. The underlying observation behind it is real. The precision is not. It comes from a small body of work, using different measurement methods on different populations, with variation between individual women that dwarfs the tidy number quoted from it. The honest version is a direction, not a decimal.

What can be said fairly is this. Collagen density falls gradually across adult life, and there is reasonable evidence that the years around menopause are a steeper section of that slope rather than a separate cliff. It is the same process running faster for a while. If you want the underlying mechanism in more detail, we have written about the slow arithmetic of collagen loss at greater length, and it sits inside the broader picture of how skin ages rather than replacing it.

What you see is a consequence of less structural support underneath. Skin looks thinner and reads as less springy. Lines that used to settle overnight now hold their shape. The jaw softens and the cheek loses a little of its lift. Texture on the neck can take on the fine crumpled look people call crepey. None of this is sudden, though it is often noticed suddenly, usually in unflattering light in a hotel bathroom.

Oil, water, and the tight feeling by mid-afternoon

Sebum production tends to fall in this period, and for many women it falls quite sharply. There is a particular disorientation in it for anyone who spent two decades managing shine. The skin goes matte, which is briefly satisfying, and then it goes tight. The oil that was such a nuisance was doing real work, sitting on the surface and slowing the rate at which water left the skin all day.

Two quite different things get called dry skin, and confusing them wastes a lot of money. One is a shortage of oil. The other is a shortage of water in the outer layers, which can happen on any skin at any age, including oily skin. The distinction between dryness and dehydration matters more now than it did before, because perimenopause can hand you both at once, which is why a single rich cream sometimes only half solves it.

The most useful early change is usually subtraction rather than addition. A foaming cleanser that was fine at thirty-five can strip a skin producing less oil at forty-eight. Very hot water does the same. Before adding a fifth step, take away the thing that leaves your face feeling squeaky, because squeaky is the sound of a barrier that has just been stripped of the lipids holding its water in.

Skin that used to tolerate everything

The other common complaint is reactivity. Products that lived on the shelf for years begin to sting. Skin flushes more easily, stays flushed longer, and takes its time settling after a wind, a hot room or a glass of wine. A barrier that recovers more slowly is less forgiving, and the acid you used twice a week without a thought is now doing more than you intended.

There is a limit to what this article can responsibly say here. Persistent redness, a rash, burning that does not settle, a patch that behaves differently from the skin around it, any change in a mole: these are not skincare questions. Several genuine medical conditions present as what people call sensitive skin, they are diagnosed by a doctor, and they are treated by a doctor. Reaching for a gentler serum instead of an appointment is the wrong instrument for the job.

For ordinary reactivity, the approach is dull and it works. Strip the routine back to a cleanser, a moisturiser and a sunscreen for a fortnight. Reintroduce one active at a time, with a week between, patch tested on the jawline first. You are gathering information about a skin that has genuinely changed, which means old knowledge about what suits you may simply be out of date.

What skincare can honestly do

Topical skincare works on the outer layers of the skin and on appearance. That is a real limit, and it is also less trivial than it sounds. A great deal of what reads on a face as tired is happening in the top fraction of a millimetre: water content in the surface layer, the evenness of the texture, how light scatters off it rather than reflecting cleanly. Change those and the face looks different, because looking different is what those things are for.

The categories with the longest-standing evidence behind them are unglamorous. Daily sun protection is the largest lever anyone has over how skin looks in ten years. A moisturiser that supports the barrier, with humectants to hold water and lipids to slow its escape, addresses the complaint most women in this decade actually have. Retinoids remain among the best-evidenced ingredients for the appearance of lines and texture over months, with a real cost in irritation, and irritation is what a reactive skin has least appetite for. Vitamin C and niacinamide sit in the sensible middle: worth using, oversold everywhere.

Here is the honest limit of all of it. Nothing you apply to the surface of your face addresses the hormonal change underneath. A cream does not restore oestrogen signalling in the dermis, and any brand implying that its formula balances your hormones has left the territory of cosmetics entirely. What good skincare does is change how skin looks and feels while the transition happens on its own schedule. That is worth doing. It is not the same thing as treating it.

Where the line sits

There are medical approaches to this transition, they are well established, and they are not our subject. They belong in a consulting room with someone who knows your history and can weigh benefit against risk for you specifically. A skincare house has no business steering that decision in either direction, and any that does should be read with suspicion.

It is also worth acknowledging how thin the skin-specific research is compared with the confidence people express about it. Much of what exists is small, short, and run on volunteers who do not much resemble the general population. The broad direction of travel is reasonably certain. The details, the timings, the individual variation, are far less settled than the marketing suggests.

The parts of this that most affect how skin looks may not be cosmetic at all. Sleep that fragments, months of it, shows on a face more plainly than any serum corrects. So do the temperature swings that interrupt it. Those are worth raising with a doctor for their own sake, and your skin is one more reason to.

A routine that changes when you do

Start by accepting that the category you have filed yourself under for twenty years may no longer apply. Plenty of women in this decade are still using products built for a skin type they no longer have, which is worth reassessing honestly rather than assuming. Combination skin becomes dry skin. Oily skin becomes normal skin that is dehydrated by the routine designed to keep it in check.

Extend whatever you do below the jaw. The neck, chest and hands show this stretch of years earlier and more plainly than the face does, partly because there is less oil and thinner skin to begin with, and partly because thirty years of diligent facial sunscreen have often stopped at the chin. It costs nothing to carry the same products further down.

Then be patient in a way the industry does not encourage. Skin turnover takes weeks and dermal change takes considerably longer, so judge a routine over seasons rather than a fortnight, and photograph yourself in the same light if you want to see anything real. The mirror is a poor instrument for measuring slow change, and a worse one at seven in the morning.

That is the claim we can stand behind. The part happening underneath is yours and your doctor's, and no cream has a legitimate opinion about it.
