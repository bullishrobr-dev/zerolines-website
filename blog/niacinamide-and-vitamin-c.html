Niacinamide and Vitamin C Together: Where the Warning Came From

Last updated: 5 October 2026 6 min read Free skin analysis · 4 min

Ask the question anywhere and you will get a firm answer within seconds, usually a warning. They cancel each other out. They turn into niacin and make your face red. Keep them apart, one in the morning, one at night. The warning was not invented from nothing, and there is real chemistry underneath it. But almost everything that makes it true in a flask stops being true in a jar.

In short

  • The warning has real chemistry underneath it. Amides hydrolyse in acid, so niacinamide can partly convert back to nicotinic acid, which is a vasodilator — and the two compounds can associate into a coloured complex.
  • The conditions that produced those findings were a flask: unbuffered water, both compounds loose in solution, sustained heat, and hours. Reaction rates roughly double for every ten degrees, so a bench hour is not a bathroom minute.
  • A finished cream is a managed environment — buffers holding the pH, chelators locking up trace metals, a smaller water phase, packaging that keeps out air. Much of the vitamin C sold now is a derivative that sits near neutral pH anyway.
  • The caveat that survives: some people feel a brief flush when niacinamide goes onto a still-wet, high-strength, low-pH ascorbic acid serum. Nothing is lost by separating them — and nothing suggests you have been getting away with something if you have not.

Where the warning comes from

Niacinamide is nicotinamide, the amide form of vitamin B3, and by most measures one of the least dramatic and most useful things you can put on skin. Vitamin C in its purest cosmetic form is L-ascorbic acid, which needs an acidic environment, somewhere near pH 3, to get anywhere at all. Set those two facts beside each other and you have the seed of the problem.

Amides hydrolyse in acid. Niacinamide is the amide of nicotinic acid, better known as niacin, and in warm acidic water some of it will convert back. Nicotinic acid is a vasodilator. Apply enough of it and you get flushing, the hot blotchy sort that anyone who has taken a high-dose niacin supplement will recognise. So: acid plus niacinamide, given heat and time, can produce something that turns a face red. That part is true.

The second strand is colour. Ascorbic acid and nicotinamide can associate in solution to form a coloured complex, an observation recorded in the chemistry literature decades before either compound was sold in a dropper bottle. A clear mixture goes yellow. To anyone watching, that looks like two ingredients destroying one another, and the interpretation stuck.

The conditions that produced it

What rarely travels alongside the warning is the environment those observations required. Water, largely unbuffered. Both compounds loose in solution rather than held in an emulsion. Heat, and not room temperature either, but the sustained warmth you apply deliberately on a bench to make a slow reaction go fast enough to measure. And time counted in hours.

That last point does most of the work. Hydrolysis of this kind is not a switch, it is a rate. A rough rule of thumb in chemical kinetics has reaction rates doubling for every ten degrees Celsius. Run that backwards from a hotplate to a bathroom shelf in Marbella and a conversion that took an hour in the flask stretches into a span of time that no longer describes anything happening on your face.

None of the original work was wrong. It was answering a different question, which was what these two molecules do to each other under conditions chosen specifically to force the issue. The answer was then repeated, for decades afterwards, as though it described a Tuesday morning.

A finished cream is not a beaker

The warning has real chemistry underneath it. Amides hydrolyse in acid, so niacinamide can partly convert back to nicotinic acid, which is a vasodilator — and the two compounds can associate into a coloured complex.

A cosmetic formulation is a managed environment, and managing exactly this sort of thing is most of what a formulator does. There are buffers holding the pH where it was set. There are chelators locking up the trace metal ions that catalyse the reactions nobody wants. The water phase is often smaller than people assume, and much of what remains is bound up in an emulsion rather than free to react. Packaging keeps out air and light.

More to the point, a great deal of the vitamin C on shelves today is not ascorbic acid at all. Magnesium ascorbyl phosphate, sodium ascorbyl phosphate, ethylated and glucoside forms all sit comfortably near neutral pH, which removes the acid half of the concern before it starts. Which one you own is usually legible from the ingredient list, if you know how to read one properly.

Then there is contact time. In a stability study the two compounds sit together for weeks. On skin, a serum dries in a minute or two, and what remains is a thin film at the skin's own mildly acidic pH, already diluted by whatever it met on the way down. The window in which anything interesting could happen is short, and the temperature is that of a face rather than a hotplate.

The colour question

People do sometimes see a serum go yellow after layering and take it as confirmation that the warning was right. Two different colours are being confused here. The tint that appears when nicotinamide and ascorbic acid meet is a complex forming between them, and it says surprisingly little about how much active is left in the mixture. It is not the same event as a bottle quietly going amber over a month.

What actually degrades vitamin C is dull and well understood: oxygen, water, light, warmth and trace metal ions, working steadily over time. Those are the reasons a serum turns orange in the bottle, and none of them need niacinamide to be present. If you want one explanation for why your vitamin C stopped looking the way it did on day one, the air in the bottle is a better suspect than the thing you layered over it.

Colour is a weak instrument in both directions. A mixture can tint without meaningful loss of activity, and activity can be lost with barely any visible change at all. We would not read much into a shade, and we would be cautious of anyone who reads a great deal into it while selling the remedy.

The caveat that honestly survives

Here is the part it would be dishonest to drop. Some people do feel a brief flush when they press niacinamide straight onto a still-wet, high-strength, low-pH ascorbic acid serum. Whether that is a trace of nicotinic acid forming on the skin, or simply a low pH on a face that was already primed to react, is not something anyone has cleanly settled. Both explanations fit what people report, and the honest limit of this is that we do not know which is doing the work.

It is a matter of how skin looks and feels for a short while, and it passes. A first week that stings is not automatically a problem, and it is not automatically fine either. Skin often does settle into a product that troubled it at first, though "it will settle" is a claim people make far too readily on someone else's behalf. If the same reaction turns up three times, the sensible response is to change something rather than wait it out.

And the ordinary caveat applies. Redness or flushing that keeps returning regardless of what you have applied is a conversation for a doctor, not something to solve by rearranging bottles.

If you would rather keep them apart

Nothing is lost by separating them, which is part of why the advice has survived so comfortably. Vitamin C in the morning and niacinamide at night is a perfectly good routine, and so is applying one, letting it dry properly, and then applying the other. The order you apply things in does real work, more than most people expect, and putting the acid-dependent active on clean dry skin first is sound practice whether or not you believe a word of the warning.

What we would not do is treat separation as a rule you have broken. If you have been using a niacinamide serum and a vitamin C serum together for two years and your skin looks fine, your skin is the evidence. Nothing in the chemistry suggests you have been getting away with something.

What we would actually say

Can you use niacinamide and vitamin C together? In nearly every case, yes. The narrower true statement is this: in acidic water, under sustained heat, given hours, niacinamide will partly convert to nicotinic acid, and the two compounds can associate into a coloured complex. Those are real findings. They describe a flask. They were never a good description of two serums meeting on a cheek for ninety seconds.

We are wary, though, of replacing one confident claim with another. The current consensus that the pairing is entirely fine rests less on a decisive body of evidence than on the reasonable observation that the old objection does not transfer to modern conditions. That is a good enough reason to stop worrying about it. It is not the same as proof, and we would rather write it down that way than pretend the question is closed.
