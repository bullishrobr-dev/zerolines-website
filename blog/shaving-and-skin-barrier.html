Shaving and the Skin Barrier: A Formulator's View

Last updated: 18 January 2027 7 min read Free skin analysis · 4 min

Most people who shave do it three or four times a week and never once file it under exfoliation. It belongs there. A razor is a blade drawn across skin under pressure, and hair is not the only thing it takes off. If you also use an acid, or a retinoid, or a weekly peel, this matters. Not because shaving is bad for your skin, but because you are almost certainly counting wrong.

In short

  • A razor is an exfoliation nobody counts. A blade drawn across skin under pressure takes hair and some of the outermost layer with it, three or four times a week.
  • So do not exfoliate the same patch of skin on the day you shave it. If you shave most mornings, the peel belongs on an evening after a day you skipped.
  • Softening does more than the blade does. Hydrated keratin is substantially easier to cut, which means fewer passes — so shave at the end of a shower, or hold a warm wet flannel there for a minute or two first.
  • What reduces irritation, in order of how much it matters: a sharp blade, light pressure, fewer passes, short strokes, rinsing between them. An aftershave that stings is a sensation, not a treatment.

What a blade actually takes off

A razor is a hard edge held at an angle and drawn across a surface that is not flat. Hair grows out of follicles sitting below that surface, so to cut a hair close to the skin the edge has to travel along the skin itself. It does not hover. What ends up rinsed down the sink is hair and, with it, some of the outermost layer of skin: the stratum corneum, a few dozen cells thick, made of flattened dead cells that were on their way off anyway.

How much? This is where honesty is more useful than confidence. It depends on the blade, the pressure, the number of passes, how hydrated the skin was and whose skin it is. Nobody can hand you a figure that would mean anything for your own face on a Tuesday morning. What can be said is that the effect is real, repeated, and comparable in kind to a gentle physical exfoliant, which is a strange thing to do four times a week without noticing.

The removal is also not selective. A blade cannot tell a cell that has finished its work from one still holding water in. An enzyme or an acid can, more or less. A razor cannot. That does not make shaving harmful, because the stratum corneum is built to shed and rebuild and it does exactly that. It means the skin is doing repair work on a schedule you set, and it would help to know you set it.

The exfoliation you are not counting

Add it up. Four shaves a week. A weekly peel or acid treatment. A retinoid most evenings. A body scrub on Sunday. Each of those is a nudge to the surface, and the skin does not file them separately. It registers only that the outer layer keeps being interrupted before it has finished settling.

This journal has argued for a while that weekly exfoliation does more for skin than daily scrubbing, and the argument holds here with one adjustment. If you shave regularly, part of that weekly allowance is already spent. Not all of it: a shave takes off less than a proper exfoliating treatment, and it only touches the area you shaved. But it is not zero, and treating it as zero is how people end up wondering why an acid that was fine last month now stings.

The practical version is unglamorous. Do not exfoliate the same patch of skin on the same day you shave it. If you shave your face most mornings, put the peel on an evening after a day you skipped. If you shave your legs in the shower, that is not the shower for the body scrub.

Softening does more than the blade does

Hair is mostly keratin, and keratin takes up water. Hydrated hair is substantially softer and easier to cut than dry hair. This is one of the better established things in the whole subject and it is the largest lever most people are not pulling. A softer hair needs less force at the edge. Less force means fewer passes. Fewer passes means less of the skin surface comes away with the hair.

In practice that means a few minutes, not a few seconds. Shave at the end of a shower rather than the start. Or hold a warm, wet flannel against the area for a minute or two beforehand and let it actually work. Splashing water on your face and reaching straight for the razor is doing very little.

Shaving creams, gels and oils are useful, and their useful job is narrower than the packaging suggests. They provide slip, so the edge glides rather than drags, and they slow the water leaving the hair while you work. That is worth having. It is not worth agonising over. The gap between a good preparation and a mediocre one is much smaller than the gap between softened skin and unsoftened skin.

Where the razor sits in the order

A razor is an exfoliation nobody counts. A blade drawn across skin under pressure takes hair and some of the outermost layer with it, three or four times a week.

Cleanse first. A blade dragged across a day of sebum, sunscreen and city air is a blade that skips and clogs, and you will compensate for that with pressure. But the choice of cleanser matters more than the order does. If your face is tight and squeaking before you have even picked up the razor, that is the wrong cleanser to be shaving behind. Tightness is the barrier reporting that it has been stripped, and stripped skin does not need a blade next.

Afterwards, the surface is briefly more permeable than usual. Water leaves it faster and whatever you apply gets in more readily, which sounds like an opportunity and is mostly a trap. This is the wrong moment for the strongest product you own. It is a good moment for something plain: a light, unfragranced hydrating layer, and then whatever order you normally apply things in, with the actives moved later or moved off the day altogether.

Across the day, the cleanest separation is the one this journal already recommends for other reasons, which is letting the morning and the evening do different jobs. Shave in the morning. Keep the acids and the retinoid for the night. Twelve hours is not a magic number, but it is enough for the surface to have stopped complaining.

What actually reduces irritation

In rough order of how much difference each one makes: a sharp blade, light pressure, fewer passes, short strokes, and rinsing the blade between them. None of that is interesting and all of it matters more than anything you can buy to put on afterwards.

A dull blade is the most common cause of a bad shave and the easiest thing to fix. It needs more force to cut the same hair, which means more pressure on the skin, which means more of the surface comes away and more of the hair gets tugged rather than cut. Replacing the blade more often than feels necessary is the cheapest improvement available anywhere in this article.

Blade count is oversold. More edges do mean each one can do less work, but they also mean more edges crossing the same patch of skin in a single stroke. That is a trade, not a free upgrade, and people whose skin is easily irritated often do better with fewer. Direction is a similar trade. With the grain is gentler, against the grain is closer, and very few people need the closest possible result every single day.

Two things help less than their marketing implies. Stretching the skin taut to get closer lifts the hair so it is cut below the surface line, which is precisely the geometry that causes some people trouble a few days later. And an aftershave that stings is not disinfecting anything. Alcohol on a surface you have just abraded is a sensation, not a treatment.

Legs, underarms and everything else

None of this is about faces particularly. The skin does not know which part of the body it is on, or whose. The same blade physics applies to a shin, an underarm, a chest, a scalp.

Legs take the largest number of passes over the largest area, usually at the end of a shower, which is accidentally the right time. The skin and the hair have had ten minutes of warm water by then. Most complaints about leg shaving come from doing it dry, or first thing, or with a blade that has been sitting in a wet shower rack for six weeks. The rack does more harm to the blade than the shaving does to you.

Underarms are the harder case. That skin is thinner, it sits in a fold, it is covered by clothing all day, it is in constant friction, and most people apply an antiperspirant to it within about ninety seconds of shaving. If that stings, it is not a sign of anything working. It is a product arriving on a surface that has just been exfoliated. Leaving a gap, or shaving at night and applying in the morning, removes most of the problem for most people.

The arithmetic from earlier applies to bodies too. A scrub, a self tan, a retinoid body lotion and a shave in the same evening is four interventions on one surface. Choose one.

Where this stops being a routine question

Some things a shaving technique will not fix. Persistent bumps along the jaw or the bikini line, hairs that curl back into the skin, inflammation that does not settle within a day. These have proper names, real causes, and answers that come from a doctor rather than from a bathroom cabinet. It is worth saying plainly, because there is a whole shelf of products sold on the implication that they are a skincare problem. They are not.

Short of that, the general rule holds. If your skin has reached the point where everything you apply stings, shaving belongs on the list of things to pause, alongside the acid and the retinoid. Pausing it is not a defeat. It is a week.

The narrow, true version of all this: shaving is not bad for your skin. It is an exfoliation you were not counting, performed with a tool that cannot be selective, on skin that is usually less prepared than it should be. Improve the preparation, count the exfoliation honestly, and replace the blade more often. That is most of it. The rest is a question of what you put on afterwards, and less of that matters than the industry would like you to think.
